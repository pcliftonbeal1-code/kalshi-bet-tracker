# Public-works paperwork beats permits as your wedge

**Permit expediting for small Philadelphia contractors is not the best starting niche. A better one is weekly "certified payroll" paperwork for small subcontractors on public (prevailing-wage) construction jobs, sold first to the small and minority-owned firms that work on City, School District, SEPTA and PennDOT projects.** The permit idea breaks in three places. First, Philadelphia law says anyone paid to represent someone else before Licenses & Inspections (L&I) must hold a personal expediter's license, which contradicts the "no license" promise. Second, licensed contractors can already file their own permits, and small remodelers pull too few permits for a monthly account. Third, a free City tool and a ~$91M-funded startup (PermitFlow) are already eating the checklist part. Certified payroll scored highest (15 of 16) of the 52 niches scored here. It is required by law every week. No license is needed, because the contractor signs the legal statement, not the operator. Quality is easy to check: the City accepts the report or sends it back. The buyers sit on public lists, and they need a ladder of related paperwork (pay applications, lien waivers, insurance certificates, license renewals, minority-business recertification) that can grow into more accounts and other regions. Two warnings matter. This ranking comes from desk research only, with no customer interviews, no published price for done-for-you certified payroll, and no count of local buyers. Many official City pages could not be opened directly, so several Philadelphia facts come from search-engine summaries and need checking. The two-week plan at the end is built to confirm or kill this wedge with real conversations and one real week of work.

## The permit idea breaks on a Philadelphia license rule

The biggest problem is legal. Philadelphia Code Chapter 9-2200 bars anyone from working as an expediter without a license, and it defines an expediter as a person **employed or paid to represent another** before L&I ([City expediter webinar, 2024](https://www.phila.gov/media/20240320143040/Licensed-Expediters-webinar.pdf)). City guidance says the license belongs to the individual, not the company, and is required **even when helping someone for free**, unless an exception applies ([phila.gov expediter license page](https://www.phila.gov/services/permits-violations-licenses/get-a-license/trade-licenses/get-an-expediters-license/)). The license itself is not hard to get. There is no exam that the research could find, just a fee, errors-and-omissions (E&O) insurance, a PA State Police background check, and being current on City taxes. The sources disagree on the fee: $200 in the code text ([§9-2203](https://codelibrary.amlegal.com/codes/philadelphia/latest/philadelphia_pa/0-0-0-279271)), $20 plus $232 a year in the webinar, and $253 in the 2024 fee schedule ([L&I fee schedule](https://www.phila.gov/media/20240903113807/li-regs-permit-license-fee-increase-schedule-amendment-2024-09-20.pdf)). In plain terms, every "everyday operator" who files for clients in Philadelphia would need their own license and would be personally accountable to the City. The "operator owns the account" model does not survive that unchanged.

The exemptions make demand weaker too. **Licensed contractors may apply for permits for work they perform or supervise without an expediter license**, and so may attorneys, architects, engineers and real estate agents within their practice ([§9-2202](https://codelibrary.amlegal.com/codes/philadelphia/latest/philadelphia_pa/0-0-0-279264)). So your target buyer already has the legal right and the online system (eCLIPSE) to do this work. You would be selling time and know-how, not access. And the small contractor is the wrong buyer for a monthly account. A third-party review says even PermitFlow fits poorly for contractors who pull only a handful of permits a year ([fieldservicesoftware.io](https://fieldservicesoftware.io/software/permitflow/)). Local expediters price **per project, at roughly $500 to $7,500+**, and no monthly retainers were found ([Permit Place cost guide](https://permitplace.com/permit-expediter-cost-guide/)).

The pain is real, but it sits where operators can't help. The worst delay is the Zoning Board: about **180 days** for a regular hearing, about 140 with a paid speed-up, and 600–650 appeals in the queue as of March 2026 ([Zoning Appeal Manual 2026](https://www.phila.gov/media/20260319105315/Zoning-Appeal-Manual-2026.1.pdf)). Lawyers and architects own that work, and no checklist makes the board meet faster. The easy part, figuring out which permits you need, is being given away. The City's free Permit Navigator now covers **all 190 property-related requirements** ([phila.gov, Mar 2026](https://www.phila.gov/2026-03-12-how-the-permit-navigator-is-simplifying-philadelphias-permitting-process/)). PermitFlow raised a **$54M Series B in December 2025**, about $91M in total ([Business Wire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20251202551013/en)), and already publishes Philadelphia and Montgomery County guides. Cities elsewhere use AI to pre-screen plans: Honolulu reports cutting residential review from 60–90 minutes to 15–20 per application ([Construction Owners](https://www.constructionowners.com/news/cities-turn-to-ai-to-speed-up-permit-reviews)).

Permits do survive in a narrower form. The suburbs are split into roughly **350 separate municipalities** across the 9-county region ([DVRPC](https://www.dvrpc.org/news/2025/new-resources-for-municipalities/)), each with its own forms, fees and portals or paper, and the research found no expediter license there. A "permit desk" for mid-size HVAC, electrical, plumbing, roofing and solar contractors who pull 10+ permits a month across many townships fits the model. Philadelphia filings would go through a licensed lead (possibly the founder), and operators would do prep work only. That ranks fourth below. It is a good later add-on for the same trade-contractor customer, not the best first door.

## Ten niches survive the first cut, and one leads

Across all five research notes, one pattern decides which niches fit: the model works best where the job is **steady, checklist-driven follow-up with a clear yes/no result** (paid, accepted, approved, filed), and worst where the job is writing text or making judgment calls. Construction billing shows why chasing matters: a perfectly prepared pay application still cannot be paid while one subcontractor's signed waiver is missing ([GetBuilt](https://getbuilt.com/blog/aia-g702-pay-application/)). Text-only work (grants, appeal letters, proposals) fails because buyers can do it with a $50–$150/month AI tool ([Grantable](https://grantable.co/how-much-does-a-grant-writer-cost)). Data-matching work (Amazon refunds, bookkeeping) fails because software or the platform itself does it. What lasts is chasing people and portals that aren't automated, with a human who is accountable.

### 1. Certified payroll for small public-works subcontractors: the lead wedge

What it is, simply: on government-funded construction, workers must be paid set minimum rates ("prevailing wage"). Every week, each contractor must file a sworn payroll report showing each worker's job type, hours and pay. On City of Philadelphia jobs, **contractors must submit weekly payroll through a system called LCPtracker, no later than 7 days after the workweek ends**, routed through the main contractor ([phila.gov](https://www.phila.gov/services/working-jobs/worker-protections/wage-protections/report-a-prevailing-wage-violation/); [Phila. Code §17-107](https://codelibrary.amlegal.com/codes/philadelphia/latest/philadelphia_pa/0-0-0-295465)). Pennsylvania prevailing-wage jobs have the same weekly rhythm ([PA DLI FAQ](https://www.pa.gov/agencies/dli/resources/compliance-laws-and-regulations/labor-management-relations/labor-law/prevailing-wage/prevailing-wage-faqs)). Enforcement is real. The City's report for January–June 2026 lists **81 prevailing-wage violations** ([DOL compliance report](https://www.phila.gov/media/20260916171415/dol-labor-policy-compliance-report-jan-june-2026.pdf), seen as a search snippet only).

Why it fits all eight rules: it is done online; no license is needed to prepare the forms, because the contractor's officer signs the Statement of Compliance (an inference from how the form works; confirm with counsel); the rules are written down, so software can turn them into steps; quality is checked by whether the report is accepted; and it repeats every week. There is already real spending. **Software alone costs $145/month for up to 5 projects** ([LCPtracker/LCPcertified](https://lcptracker.com/?p=101)), **$375/month** for a post-payroll plan ([Points North](https://apps.adp.com/en-us/apps/248331/points-north-certified-payroll-reporting-for-adp-workforce-now/configure)), and **$8,000/year for 1–5 jobs** ([Dapt](https://apps.adp.com/en-US/apps/794477/dapt-certified-payroll-prevailing-wage-processing-for-adp-workforce-now/configure)), and that is before anyone does the work. You can find the buyers without connections. The City publishes contract awards, the City's Office of Labor Standards runs LCPtracker trainings, and the OEO registry of certified minority-, women- and disabled-owned firms is public ([phila.gov OEO directory](https://phila.gov/services/business-self-employment/find-a-minority-woman-or-disabled-owned-business)). Those certified firms received **31% of the City's $1.2B in contract spending** in FY23 ([City Controller](https://controller.phila.gov/philadelphia-reports/may-2025-municipal-money-matters/)). Many are small subcontractors with no payroll department, and they also face yearly recertification ($200–$500 through the Enterprise Center) and City tax and license upkeep ([Enterprise Center](https://theenterprisecenter.com/business-services/mwdsbe-certification)). That gives you a natural bundle.

Stress-tested to its breaking point, it bends in five places. **The competition is funded and arriving.** Dili raised $15M in July 2026 to check every line of WH-347 payroll forms against prevailing-wage rules automatically ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/30/dili-raises-15-million-to-bring-ai-compliance-to-the-infrastructure-boom/)). Miter has raised $78M in total for construction payroll ([Construction Dive](https://www.constructiondive.com/press-release/20260930-miter-the-ai-platform-for-construction-raises-40-million-for-the-built-w/)). Trayd and Lumber (which bought Pivla) are in the same space ([Lumber](https://www.lumberfi.com/pivla-acquisition)). These are software sold to general contractors and payroll companies. The "someone does it for me" layer for a 10-person subcontractor looks thin, but that is an inference, not verified. If a sub switches to one of these payroll platforms, your job shrinks to chasing missing data and uploading. **The government is making the form easier**: the U.S. Department of Labor released a fillable WH-347 with an annotated guide in December 2025 ([DOL](https://www.dol.gov/newsroom/releases/whd/whd20251216-0)). **A mistake costs real money**: putting a worker in the wrong job class means back pay is owed, so you need E&O insurance and a careful review step. **The size of the buyer pool is unknown**: the research could not find how many subs file to the City's LCPtracker. **A sub with only one public job may not pay $300+/month**, and done-for-you pricing was never found. What survives: subs and small primes with two or more active prevailing-wage jobs, who use QuickBooks or ADP and won't switch payroll systems, priced per active job per week. The scale story is national: federal Davis-Bacon rules cover federally funded construction everywhere, and many states have their own prevailing-wage laws (the exact number of states was not verified here).

### 2. Pay applications and lien waivers for the same subcontractors

Every month, each subcontractor on each active project sends a bill to the general contractor (a "pay application"). It must come with signed papers saying it has been paid for earlier work and won't claim the property (a "lien waiver"). **One missing waiver can hold a whole billing cycle for 2–4 weeks** ([Levelset](https://www.levelset.com/blog/checklist-contractors-documents-submit-with-pay-application/)), and subcontractors wait an average of **96 days** to get paid ([Siteline report via CB Insights](https://www.cbinsights.com/company/siteline/alternatives-competitors)). It is chase-heavy and monthly, and the result is easy to check: was the bill accepted and paid on time? Because the buyer is the same as in #1, it is the natural second account. It breaks in two places. No public price for an outsourced pay-app service was found, so willingness to pay $300+/month is unproven. And general contractors' own portals standardize the paperwork, some for free (Trimble Pay is free for subs when a GC invites them, per [Trimble](https://www.trimble.com/en/products/trimble-pay/)), while Siteline ($18.4M raised) sells software for this to subs ([Business Wire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20220224005099/en/Siteline-Raises-$18.4M-to-Reimagine-Construction-Finance)). Some steps need notarization, which may affect the "fully remote" rule (remote notarization rules in PA were not checked). An idea worth testing: many small clients buy these tools and then have no one to run them, so "we run your Siteline/Procore paperwork" is a service angle.

### 3. Credentialing for small medical and therapy practices: the strongest backup

Doctors, therapists and dentists must be approved ("credentialed") by each insurance company and keep their records current. Maintenance services charge **$100–$500 per provider per month** ([Carepatron](https://www.carepatron.com/blog/how-much-does-credentialing-cost)), and RCM Matter charges $199 ([RCM Matter](https://rcmmatter.com/credentialing-services)). It has the best-proven prices of any niche here, the work is portals and phone follow-up, the result is binary (approved or not), and the job is commonly done by remote staff without licenses. It breaks on three things. **Funded AI competitors**: Assured raised a $19M Series A in July 2026 ([Fierce Healthcare](https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/finance/startup-assured-banks-19m-series-ai-native-credientialing-provider-enrollment)), and Medallion raised $43M in August 2025 ([FinSMEs](https://www.finsmes.com/2025/08/medallion-raises-43m-in-new-funding.html)). **Bundling**: Carepatron includes credentialing for up to 5 insurers free with its $99/month billing ([Carepatron](https://www.carepatron.com/blog/medical-credentialing-services)). **Privacy law**: practices will demand a HIPAA business associate agreement, which makes loosely managed operators risky ([HHS](https://hhs.gov/ocr/privacy/hipaa/understanding/coveredentities/businessassociates.html)). Reaching practices is also harder for a 19-year-old with no network, because office managers act as gatekeepers. It is the best Plan B if construction buyers won't pay. It is also the least Philadelphia-specific, which helps scale but removes the local advantage.

### 4. A suburban-plus-Philadelphia permit desk for mid-volume trade contractors

This is the original idea, narrowed until it holds. The target is HVAC, electrical, plumbing, roofing and solar firms that pull 10+ permits a month across Philadelphia and the collar-county townships, where every township is different. Pennsylvania has no county building department, and certified private agencies such as Building Inspection Underwriters handle inspections for many towns across Bucks, Chester, Delaware and Montgomery counties ([PA L&I listing](https://www.pa.gov/content/dam/copapwp-pagov/en/dli/documents/ucc/buildings/documents/third-party-agencies-(buildings)/building-inspection-underwriters.pdf)). A per-township checklist library is exactly what AI plus checklists does well, and it takes years to copy. It breaks on four things: the Philadelphia license rule, paper and in-person steps in some townships (unverified per town), unknown permit volume per contractor, and simple standard permits (solar, heat pumps) heading toward instant approval. Philadelphia's solar EZ permit already clears **within 3 business days online** ([phila.gov](https://www.phila.gov/services/permits-violations-licenses/apply-for-a-permit/building-and-repair-permits/get-a-permit-to-install-solar-panels/)). The City's open permit data includes a "Permit Contractors" file ([OpenDataPhilly](https://opendataphilly.org/datasets/licenses-and-inspections-building-and-zoning-permits/)), so you can find who pulls the most permits in an afternoon. That data could not be pulled in this research.

### 5. Housing-voucher (Section 8) and rental-license paperwork, sold to property managers

Philadelphia has about **55,000 landlords owning 288,000 rental units** ([Pew](https://www.pewtrusts.org/research-and-analysis/articles/2021/02/24/who-are-philadelphias-landlords)). In 2020, about 45% of rental properties were unlicensed ([Pew 2021](https://www.pewtrusts.org/research-and-analysis/reports/2021/11/rental-code-enforcement-in-philadelphia)). Landlords must renew a rental license every year ($69 per unit) and keep a lead-safe certificate that expires after 4 years ([phila.gov](https://www.phila.gov/services/permits-violations-licenses/get-a-license/business-licenses/rental-and-property/get-a-rental-license/)). Landlords who rent to voucher holders must also handle Philadelphia Housing Authority leasing packets, inspections and recertifications, under a new Administrative Plan that took effect January 1, 2026 ([PHA](https://www.pha.phila.gov/landlords/landlord-responsibilities/); [PHA Admin Plan](https://www.pha.phila.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Admin-Plan-Effective-01-01-2026.pdf)). No funded competitor was found for voucher paperwork, and HAPCO, the largest local landlord association, claims 1,500+ members ([City Landlord Gateway PDF](https://www.phila.gov/media/20231005141952/LG-HAPCO-services-for-members-2023.pdf)). It breaks on money. Most landlords own 1–5 units and will not pay $300/month. Only a licensed inspector can certify lead safety. Inspections are physical. Price and willingness to pay are unknown, so confidence here is low. It survives only if property managers with many voucher units buy it.

| 52 | Insurance back office (email triage, claims processing) | 2 | 1 | 1 | 2 | 1 | 1 | 2 | 0 | 10 | L | Pace and YC AI agencies replacing outsourcers; barely researched |

The failures fall into three groups, and each teaches something. **License walls** kill customs work, insurance certificate issuance, BEPP tune-ups, zoning, and Philadelphia city permits. The customs rule is the clearest case: classifying goods for someone else is "customs business" that legally requires a broker license ([19 CFR 111.1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/19/111.1)). **Price collapse** kills bookkeeping, sales tax and annual filings. Pilot sells an AI-only bookkeeping tier at $99/month ([Pelcro](https://www.get.pelcro.com/blog/best-online-bookkeeping-services)), and Bench, a 600-person human-heavy bookkeeping startup, shut down suddenly in December 2024 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/12/30/bench-to-be-acquired-after-abruptly-shutting-down)). **Platform or AI takeover** kills Amazon refunds and text work: Amazon cut its refund claim window to 60 days and now reimburses many losses on its own ([eComEngine](https://www.ecomengine.com/blog/fba-reimbursement-policy)). The survivors share one trait: someone has to keep chasing people and government systems every week, and the result is a plain pass or fail.

## The model itself needs three design fixes

**First, hire operators as W-2 employees, and keep them that way until a lawyer says otherwise.** Distributed contractor workforces keep getting sued for misclassification. The U.S. Department of Labor sued Arise over **more than 22,000 workers** it says were wrongly treated as independent contractors ([DOL](https://www.dol.gov/newsroom/releases/WHD/WHD20230629-1)), and a 2026 proposed class action against Mercor covers about 30,000 AI-training "experts" ([Independent Contractor Compliance blog](https://www.independentcontractorcompliance.com/2026/06/09/artificial-intelligence-firms-continue-to-be-targeted-for-independent-contractor-misclassification-claims-may-2026-ic-legal-news-update/)). The model's own features (required checklists, the company owning the client, the company setting the price) all point toward "employee." So "operators own accounts" should mean pay and continuity tied to an account, not 1099 independence. New Jersey's strict "ABC test" makes this matter even more if you expand across the river.

**Second, keep the legal signature with the client or a licensed person, and make operators preparers.** Certified payroll does this naturally, because the contractor signs. Philadelphia permits do not, because the licensed expediter is L&I's accountable contact and is warned against using a client's eCLIPSE account without authorization ([City webinar](https://www.phila.gov/media/20240320143040/Licensed-Expediters-webinar.pdf)). This is a strong reason certified payroll fits better than permits.

**Third, pitch it the way investors now want to hear it.** YC's Request for Startups asks for "AI-native agencies" that sell finished work with software-like margins ([Tech Startups summary of YC RFS](https://techstartups.com/2026/02/04/y-combinator-is-out-with-its-2026-request-for-startups/)). YC visiting partner Charlie Warren warns that **"variance is the enemy of trust"** and calls for "process evals for the entire system, including the humans in the loop" ([Warren essay](https://bearing.substack.com/p/trust-in-ai-native-service-companies)). Your checklists and operator scoring are that eval system, so lead with them. "Everyday people own accounts" can sound like a labor marketplace. "An AI-native public-works compliance firm where every report passes the same checks" sounds like what YC is asking for. Be honest about the money too. AI-native companies run about **55–70% gross margin** versus 80–85% for classic software ([SaaSRise](https://www.saasrise.com/blog/saas-benchmark-report-2026)), and paid operators push that lower. The number that proves the model is **accounts per operator rising over time**.

Defensibility will not come from the checklist itself. The City gives permit checklists away for free, and Dili automates payroll checking. It comes from three things that build up over time: a rules library covering City, state and federal prevailing-wage rules plus each agency's portal quirks; a record of accepted reports that shows you are reliable; and being the people small subs already trust to handle the next piece of paperwork.

## Two weeks to a yes-or-no on the wedge

YC's advice is to talk to users and do things that don't scale, so the plan below is mostly conversations and one real week of hand-done work. Cold email alone will be slow. A large vendor study measured **0.45% average unique replies**, though firms with 0–10 employees replied at 0.72% ([Belkins](https://belkins.io/blog/cold-email-response-rates)). So use phone calls, visits, and triggers from public data. The pass/fail thresholds below are suggestions, not research findings.

| Days | What to do | What you should have at the end |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Build a list of 100 small contractors on public jobs: OEO registry firms in construction trades, City and School District contract awards, and subs named on primes' participation plans. In parallel, pull the OpenDataPhilly "Permit Contractors" file to find firms pulling 10+ permits a month (a permit-desk control group). Book a Drexel Entrepreneurial Law Clinic appointment ([Drexel Kline ELC](https://drexel.edu/law/academics/clinics/entrepreneurial-law-clinic/)). | A named list with phone numbers (enriched from Google and websites); an ELC slot |
| 3–7 | Make 30–40 calls or visits. Ask: Who does your certified payroll today? How many hours a week? What did it cost last month? When were you last sent a correction or a violation notice? Do you also struggle with pay apps, lien waivers, COIs or recertification? Attend an LCPtracker training or City business event if one is scheduled. Get quotes from 3–5 existing certified-payroll service bureaus to learn the done-for-you price. | Notes from 30+ conversations; a real market price for done-for-you work |
| 3–7 (parallel) | Call 10 high-volume permit pullers from the control list with the same questions about permits. | A head-to-head pain comparison with the original idea |
| 8–10 | Do one real week of certified payroll by hand for 1–3 subs, free or cheap. Write every step down as a checklist. Time it. | A first checklist; real hours per account; one case study |
| 11–14 | Ask for money: a paid pilot at $300+/month or a signed letter of intent. Show the ELC your pilot agreement and operator plan. | A yes/no decision |

Suggested decision rule: **continue** if at least 10 of 30 conversations confirm weekly pain that someone is paid or stressed to handle, at least 3 say they would pay $300+/month, and at least 1 lets you do a real week. If certified payroll fails but the same subs keep raising pay apps or lien waivers, switch to that. If construction buyers fail across the board, run the same two weeks on small-practice credentialing.

## Conclusion

The search for "the right niche" turned out to be a search for the right buyer. The small trade subcontractor sits in a gap: too small for PermitFlow's enterprise contracts, and an end user rather than a buyer for Dili's and Miter's software sold to general contractors and payroll firms. That same buyer has a pile of weekly and monthly paperwork that ends in a clear pass or fail. Certified payroll is simply the most urgent, best-documented item in that pile, so it makes the best first door. Permits are not dead. They become the fifth or sixth thing you handle for a customer who already trusts you, possibly through a founder who holds the Philadelphia expediter license.

The bigger lesson is about the model itself. "Everyday people become experts" works only where the expertise can be written as rules and someone else holds the legal signature. Those two filters, more than market size, eliminated most of the 52 niches. They also point the long-term vision toward work where the rules are public, the deadlines are legal, and the result is binary. That describes much of government-facing small-business paperwork in every U.S. metro, which is where the hundreds-of-millions path, if it exists, will be found. No market size for that path was measured in this research.
